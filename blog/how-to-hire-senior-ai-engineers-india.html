How to Hire Senior AI Engineers in India: The Evidence-Based Approach

The standard hiring toolkit was built for a different era. Applying it to senior AI engineering roles produces slow pipelines, weak shortlists, and candidates who look right on paper but cannot deliver at the level the role demands.

If you are trying to hire a senior AI engineer in India through a job posting and CV review, you are selecting from the fraction of the market that is actively looking, which is not where the best engineers are. The engineers who have built and deployed production-grade ML systems, who have owned the full lifecycle from data pipeline to monitoring, are almost universally employed and not submitting applications.

This is not a supply problem. India has a substantial and growing pool of senior AI and machine learning engineers. It is a sourcing problem. The methods that surface mid-level candidates in active job markets do not work for senior AI roles, and companies that do not adjust their approach end up either waiting too long or making compromised hires.

Why the standard screening toolkit fails here

The typical screening sequence for technical roles involves CV keyword matching, a take-home coding problem, and a technical interview. For senior AI engineering roles, each step introduces a different failure mode.

CV keyword matching misses strong candidates because senior AI engineers do not write CVs the way junior engineers do. They tend to list the projects they worked on, the systems they built, and the outcomes they delivered, without necessarily including every framework name or library your recruiter is searching for. A CV that passes a keyword filter is often more carefully optimised than genuinely accomplished.

Timed algorithmic coding tests are a poor signal for senior AI competence. A coding test measures the ability to solve algorithmic problems quickly under pressure. That is occasionally relevant for certain engineering roles but is largely unrelated to the skills that make an AI engineer effective: designing and deploying ML systems, navigating data quality problems, building inference pipelines that hold up under production load, and making architecture decisions under uncertainty.

The result is a screening process that filters by the wrong signal and produces shortlists that look technically credible but are not predictive of real performance.

What production evidence actually looks like

The only reliable signal for senior AI engineering performance is production evidence: systems that have actually run at scale, delivering real business outcomes, in the conditions that senior engineers deal with every day.

Concretely, that means: machine learning models deployed in production with measurable performance metrics, not just trained in notebooks. MLOps infrastructure built and maintained by the candidate, not inherited and operated. Inference optimisation work, covering latency reduction, cost management, and serving architecture decisions. Evidence of owning the full ML lifecycle, from data sourcing and pipeline engineering through model evaluation, deployment, and monitoring. And the ability to articulate design decisions under examination, not just describe what was built.

A candidate who can walk through a specific production system, explain the design decisions they made and why, describe what broke and how they fixed it, and quantify the business impact of the system is a credible senior AI engineer. A candidate who can score well on a LeetCode problem is demonstrably something, but that something is not the same thing.

The challenge is that surfacing this evidence requires a different kind of interview than most companies run. It requires structured technical interviews built around case analysis of real systems, reference calls with people who have worked directly with the candidate, and assessment of how the candidate communicates architectural decisions to non-technical stakeholders. A data-driven candidate assessment framework built around these dimensions produces shortlists that are genuinely predictive of performance rather than just screening for proxies.

Research-led sourcing: how to reach candidates who are not looking

The sourcing challenge for senior AI engineers in India is real but solvable. The solution is not a better job posting — it is research-led outreach to candidates identified through their technical output.

Senior AI engineers leave a public record. They publish research, contribute to open-source projects, present at PyConf, NeurIPS India, or internal tech talks their company posts on YouTube, and write technical blog posts about the problems they are working on. That record is the sourcing surface for a well-run search. Identifying candidates through their documented technical work and approaching them with a specific, evidence-based brief gives you a genuine opening that a generic LinkedIn InMail does not.

The brief itself matters as much as the sourcing method. A senior AI engineer who is comfortably employed at a well-funded startup needs a compelling reason to have a conversation. That reason is not compensation alone, and it is rarely a better job title. It is typically the scale of the technical problem, the quality of the team, the architecture decisions they would get to make, or the stage and trajectory of the company. An effective sourcing conversation leads with the engineering problem, not the package.

Assessment dimensions that predict performance

Talhive's assessment of senior AI engineering candidates covers four dimensions, each designed to go beyond CV-level claims.

Technical depth: what has the candidate actually built in production, what architectural decisions did they make, and can they defend those decisions under interrogation? This is assessed through structured technical interviews and documented case analysis, not standardised tests.

Lifecycle ownership: has the candidate owned the full ML pipeline or only a component of it? Engineers who have only ever trained models but never deployed them, monitored them, or debugged production failures are not senior AI engineers in the sense the role demands.

Communication and influence: can the candidate explain technical decisions to non-technical stakeholders, align with product and business on prioritisation, and manage up when technical constraints conflict with business timelines? At senior level, this is not a soft skill — it is a core competency.

Reference validation: direct references from the candidate's former engineering manager, a peer engineer, and where possible a direct report. Reference calls are structured around specific situations, not general impressions, and they surface information that no interview process reaches.

Questions to ask in your technical interview

For hiring managers running their own technical assessment, these questions focus the conversation on evidence rather than theory. Ask the candidate to describe the most complex production ML system they have owned. Ask them what broke in production and how they diagnosed it. Ask them about a time a model degraded after deployment and what the root cause was. Ask them to walk through an inference latency problem they encountered and how they solved it. Ask them what they would do differently if they were building the same system today.

Every question is anchored to a real production experience. Candidates who have the experience will answer in specifics. Candidates who do not will generalise.

Senior AI engineering is a high-leverage hire. Getting it right shortens the time between a model being trained and it delivering business value. Getting it wrong extends that timeline by months and introduces technical debt that compounds. The sourcing and assessment approach needs to match the importance of the decision.
