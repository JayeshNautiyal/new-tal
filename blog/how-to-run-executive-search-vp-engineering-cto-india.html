How to Run an Executive Search for VP Engineering or CTO in India

A VP Engineering or CTO search in India done well takes four to five months and produces a shortlist of three to five genuinely strong candidates. Done poorly, it takes longer and produces a shortlist of none. Here is the practical guide to doing it well.

The engineering leadership search is one of the most consequential hiring decisions a company makes. The VP Engineering or CTO sets the technical direction, builds or inherits the team, and is often the single hire that determines whether an India engineering centre delivers on its promise or underperforms its mandate. Getting it right the first time matters significantly more than it does for most other roles.

This guide covers the full process from the first internal conversation about the role to the day one of a successful placement. Each phase has a specific purpose, common failure modes, and a realistic timeline.

Phase one: Brief development

The brief is the single most important input to the search. A well-developed brief makes the sourcing more precise, makes the outreach more compelling, and ensures the assessment is calibrated to what the role actually requires. A poor brief produces a pipeline of candidates who match the words in a job description rather than the reality of what the company needs.

A strong brief for a VP Engineering or CTO search in India covers: the current engineering team structure and size, what the incoming leader will inherit versus what they will need to build, the specific technical architecture decisions they will face in the first 12 months, the relationship between India engineering and the global product or engineering function, the commercial context in which the engineering team operates, and what success looks like at 12 and 24 months.

The brief should also address the profile explicitly: what prior experience is genuinely required versus preferred, what kind of environment the candidate should come from, how important prior India team-building experience is relative to technical depth, and what the salary range is based on current market data. This last point deserves its own conversation with a search partner before the search begins, because misaligned salary expectations are a leading cause of searches that close slowly or not at all.

Brief development typically takes one to two weeks when done in collaboration with a search partner. The partner's role in this phase is to surface what the brief is missing, what market data suggests about the candidate profile's realistic availability, and what adjustments would increase the probability of a strong shortlist.

Phase two: Research and sourcing

The research phase is where the sourcing population is identified. For a VP Engineering or CTO search in India, this means mapping the population of candidates who have the specific combination of team-building track record, technical leadership experience, and organisational context that the brief requires. That population is typically sourced from product companies, funded startups, and GCCs in the relevant cities.

Passive sourcing for these roles means reaching candidates who are currently employed and not actively looking. The outreach is built around the specific brief: the engineering problem, the mandate scope, the team they would be building, and what makes this opportunity worth considering despite the risk of leaving a stable position. Generic outreach does not reach passive candidates in a meaningful way. Specific, credible, brief-led outreach does.

The research and outreach phase typically runs from week two through week six. The first shortlist emerges from this phase, typically in weeks four through six, and represents the candidates who have engaged with the initial outreach and been screened by the search partner.

Phase three: Assessment

Assessment for VP Engineering and CTO roles is more complex than for individual contributor searches. It involves evaluating technical depth, leadership quality, strategic capability, and organisational fit in a single individual. Optimising for one dimension at the expense of others is a common cause of hires that initially seem strong and then fail within eighteen months.

The Talhive assessment process for engineering leadership roles includes a structured technical deep-dive conversation covering the most complex systems the candidate has built or led, the architectural decisions they made, and specific failures and recoveries. It includes a leadership conversation covering team structure, hiring and development philosophy, and how the candidate handles disagreement with peer functions. It includes a CDQ (Career Discovery Questionnaire) mapping the candidate's trajectory, motivations, and the environments in which they perform best.

References are treated as structured evidence collection rather than formality. Conversations with a former manager, a peer, and a direct report produce the third-party perspective that no amount of direct interviewing can replicate. The questions focus on specific performance dimensions: what was the hardest problem this person solved, where did they struggle, what would you change about how they handled a specific situation?

Assessment runs from weeks six through nine, concurrent with client interview stages. The goal is that by the time a candidate reaches the offer stage, the hiring team has a complete picture of their technical capability, leadership approach, motivations, and references.

Phase four: Offer and acceptance

Offer stage for senior engineering leadership searches has a higher drop-out risk than earlier stages. Candidates who are passive at the start of the process have existing packages, competing offers, and the option to stay where they are. The offer needs to be competitive on all dimensions the candidate cares about: base compensation, performance incentives, equity where applicable, and the clarity of the role mandate and authority.

Compensation should have been benchmarked before the search began. If the brief was honest about the band and the band was competitive for the market, this stage should be a closing conversation rather than a renegotiation. If the band is below market, this is when the search either loses a strong candidate or requires the hiring team to revisit their initial parameters.

Offer and acceptance typically runs weeks nine through twelve. Notice periods add two to three months for most candidates at this seniority level in India. The full cycle from brief completion to day one is typically five to six months. Companies that begin a search expecting a three-month turnaround consistently find themselves behind schedule and under pressure to accept candidates who are below the bar the brief described.

What to do when the search stalls

The most common reason an executive search stalls is a brief problem. Either the role is not defined well enough to attract the right candidates, the salary band is misaligned with market, or the interview process is taking so long that candidates lose interest or accept competing offers. A good search partner surfaces this diagnosis early and brings it to the hiring team clearly, even when the conversation is uncomfortable.

The right response to a stalling search is a brief recalibration conversation, not a broadening of the candidate criteria to accept profiles that do not actually meet the need. Accepting a weaker candidate to close the search faster solves the immediate problem and creates a larger one downstream. The engineering leadership team is too important to staff under time pressure.
