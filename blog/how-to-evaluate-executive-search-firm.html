How to Evaluate an Executive Search Firm for Technical Roles

Not all executive search firms are equipped to fill VP Engineering and CTO roles well. The evaluation questions that matter are not about firm size or brand — they are about sourcing methodology and technical assessment capability.

Selecting a search partner for a senior technical leadership role is itself a consequential decision. A poor choice costs six to twelve weeks and produces nothing useful. A good one produces a shortlist of three to five genuinely strong candidates within four to six weeks, handles the candidate relationship throughout the process, and delivers market intelligence that improves the brief along the way.

The evaluation conversation with prospective search partners should go beyond credentials and case studies. The questions below are the ones that reliably separate partners who can execute a senior technical search from those who cannot.

Question one: How do you source candidates who are not actively looking?

This is the most important question in the evaluation. The answer distinguishes a search firm that runs genuine executive search from one that queries a database of candidates who have registered or applied somewhere in the past.

A credible answer describes a research process: identifying the population of people who have done the specific work the role requires, mapping where they currently work, and approaching them directly with a specific and compelling brief. It includes an explanation of how the firm builds credibility with passive candidates who have no prior relationship with them, and how they handle the early part of that conversation when the candidate is sceptical.

An answer that centres on the firm's database, their LinkedIn Recruiter licence, or their existing network of candidates who have previously expressed interest is not an answer to the question of passive sourcing. It is an answer to the question of active candidate management, which is a different and lower-value capability for this type of search.

Question two: Who will actually work on this search?

In large executive search firms, the partner who wins the engagement and the consultant who runs it are frequently different people. This is a significant quality risk. The person who conducted the sales conversation and demonstrated industry knowledge and technical credibility may not be the person reviewing CVs and making outreach calls in week three.

Ask directly: who leads the research phase, who drafts the outreach, who conducts the initial screening conversations, and who delivers the shortlist presentation? The answer should be specific about individuals, not roles. If it is not possible to get a specific answer, or if the answer involves "a team" without naming the team members, that is a meaningful data point about how the firm operates.

Question three: Can you assess technical credibility, not just cultural fit?

A search firm conducting a VP Engineering or Head of AI search needs to be able to evaluate whether a candidate's claimed technical experience is genuine, before they appear on a shortlist. This requires the people conducting screening interviews to have enough technical understanding to ask specific questions about production systems, architecture decisions, and team-level technical leadership.

Ask the firm how they assess whether a candidate's technical background is real versus presented. A strong answer describes structured technical interview frameworks, specific lines of questioning around production systems and failure modes, and a process for separating candidates who describe work they owned from those who describe work they were adjacent to.

An answer that relies primarily on reviewing CVs and asking candidates to self-report their experience is insufficient for senior technical roles. The value of a specialist search firm is exactly that they add an assessment layer that the CV does not provide.

Question four: What happens if the first shortlist does not satisfy the brief?

This question tests the firm's commitment structure and their view of the engagement. A search that produces an unsatisfactory first shortlist is not unusual. Briefs evolve during the sourcing process as market intelligence reveals that the initial candidate profile needs adjustment. Salary bands sometimes need revision. The ideal sourcing population sometimes turns out to be different from the one the brief described.

A strong answer describes a commitment to iterate: a second round of sourcing with a recalibrated brief, regular pipeline reporting that makes problems visible early, and a fee structure that keeps the firm financially committed to the outcome rather than just the submission of profiles.

An answer that implies the work is done once profiles are submitted, or that requires a new engagement for a second shortlist, is a clear signal about accountability. The search is not complete until a strong candidate accepts an offer. The partner you want is the one who sees it that way too.

Question five: What does your assessment process look like beyond the interview?

Executive search firms add value through assessment, not just sourcing. The assessment dimension includes structured competency-based interviews, reference conversations structured around specific performance questions rather than general impressions, and in some cases psychometric tools or case-based assessments. Ask what the firm does in each of these areas and how they structure reference calls in particular.

References for senior technical roles are most valuable when they speak to the specific performance questions that matter for the role: how did this person build and develop a team, how did they handle a major technical failure, what do they struggle with, and would you hire them again into a role like this? A firm that has a structured reference methodology will be able to describe it. One that treats references as a formality will not.

The red flags to watch for in the evaluation

Several signals in the evaluation conversation indicate a partner who is unlikely to perform well on a senior technical search:

  • Claims of a "strong network" in the technical leadership space without specifics about how they reach passive candidates
  • References to large databases without a clear explanation of how those databases are kept current and relevant
  • Vague answers about who will actually run the search
  • A fee structure that front-loads payment on submission of profiles rather than on quality of shortlist or successful placement
  • Inability to describe how they assess technical credibility before a profile lands on your desk

The right partner for a senior technical search is one who treats the brief development as seriously as the sourcing, who assigns specific people with relevant experience to the mandate, and who carries accountability for the outcome of the search, not just the volume of activity.
