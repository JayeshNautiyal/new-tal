RPO vs Staffing Agency vs Recruitment Agency: How to Choose

Three different models, three different incentive structures, three different outcomes. Understanding what separates Recruitment Process Outsourcing from a staffing agency or recruitment agency is the first step to choosing the right one.

Talhive Oct 27, 2026

The three models defined clearly

Before evaluating which model fits a particular hiring situation, it is worth being precise about what each one actually is. The terms get used loosely and sometimes interchangeably, which creates confusion when companies are trying to make a decision.

A staffing agency places workers into client organisations on a contract or temporary basis. The agency maintains a pool of available workers, typically manages their employment, and charges the client a markup over the worker's day rate or salary. The agency's business is built on utilisation: keeping its workers placed and billing. The client relationship is transactional, and the agency's incentive is to deploy available workers into available slots, not to find the best person for a specific role.

A recruitment agency (sometimes called a search firm or headhunter, though those terms have specific distinctions) sources candidates for permanent roles. The most common fee model is contingency: the agency is paid only when a placement is made, typically 8 to 15 percent of the placed candidate's first-year salary. Some agencies work on an exclusive or contained basis with a portion of the fee paid upfront, but the dominant model in most markets remains contingency. The incentive is to close a placement as quickly as possible, because unplaced candidates earn nothing.

An RPO provider takes ownership of part or all of the recruitment process itself. Recruitment Process Outsourcing services are paid for capacity, milestones, or outcomes rather than individual placements. The provider operates as a functional extension of the client's team, accountable to process quality and hiring outcomes rather than placement volume. The incentive is to build a sustainable hiring capability, not to fill individual roles quickly.

How incentives shape outcomes

The incentive structure is the most important variable to understand when choosing between these models. It determines the behaviour of the people you are working with regardless of their stated values or methodology.

A staffing agency is incentivised to deploy available workers. The best available worker for a slot is not necessarily the best person for the role, but the distinction is secondary when the commercial model rewards utilisation over fit. This is not a criticism of staffing agencies; it is an accurate description of the model. Staffing works well when the primary need is coverage and availability rather than specific excellence.

A contingency recruitment agency is incentivised to close placements quickly. Every day a role is unfilled is a day closer to the client going with another agency or deciding to hire the role internally. The contingency recruiter therefore routes the most immediately available, most easily presented candidates first. For roles where there is a reasonable pool of active candidates, this can work. For roles where the best candidates are passive and require sustained outreach, the contingency model is structurally misaligned with the search.

An RPO provider is incentivised to build a process that works over the engagement period. The fee is not contingent on individual placements, so the pressure to close quickly does not distort sourcing decisions. A provider who is paid for capacity has the mandate to spend more time on a genuinely difficult search, to invest in the passive candidate relationships that produce the best results, and to surface pipeline problems early rather than masking them with activity.

Understanding this before you engage means you can match the model's incentive structure to your actual hiring situation, rather than hoping the provider will behave differently from what their commercial model rewards.

What each model does well

Each of the three models has genuine strengths in specific situations, and the honest evaluation is to match model strength to hiring need rather than applying a single model to all situations.

Staffing agencies are good at: providing contract or temporary cover for a skill gap, filling roles quickly where availability matters more than tenure, reducing risk when a permanent hire is not yet justified, and covering project-specific needs with a defined end date. For technology teams, this means contract engineers for specific builds, interim technical leads during a search for a permanent leader, and specialist contractors for one-off integrations or migrations.

Recruitment agencies (operating on contingency or exclusive basis) are good at: single senior searches where the role is well-defined and the market of potential candidates is relatively clear, searches with a tight timeline where speed is genuinely the most important variable, and niche or specialist roles where the agency has a specific market expertise and existing candidate relationships. A specialist technical agency with a deep network in a particular engineering discipline can deliver a faster shortlist for a specific search than an embedded RPO model that would take time to build that market knowledge from scratch.

RPO is good at: sustained hiring across multiple simultaneous roles, senior engineering and leadership hiring where passive sourcing and relationship building matter, building a repeatable and improving process over time, and managing the complexity of multiple concurrent searches without creating a coordination overhead for internal engineering managers. The embedded RPO model in particular delivers its strongest results when process consistency across multiple searches is as valuable as the individual hire quality.

Why engineering teams typically outgrow the agency model

The contingency agency model was designed for a different talent market than the one most technology companies face for senior engineering roles. It works reasonably well when candidates are active, the role profile is straightforward, and the primary challenge is finding available people. Senior engineering hiring in India, particularly at staff and principal level and above, is not that market.

Senior engineers are predominantly employed at other companies and not actively seeking new roles. They do not browse job boards meaningfully, and they receive enough inbound recruiter outreach that generic messages are routinely ignored. Reaching them requires identifying the right people through their technical output, crafting specific and credible outreach that speaks to their career motivations, and building a genuine conversation over days or weeks. A contingency agency working across fifteen client mandates simultaneously cannot economically justify that investment for any single search.

The second problem is coordination. As a company scales its engineering team and finds itself with four, five, or six open roles simultaneously, the agency model creates a tax on engineering managers that compounds. Reviewing high volumes of CVs from multiple agencies, coordinating interview processes across different candidate pipelines, managing the interpersonal dynamics of multiple competing vendors, and maintaining consistent candidate experience across all active searches is a significant overhead. An embedded RPO partner absorbs that coordination and brings it inside the function, where it belongs.

For companies that have reached the point where engineering manager time is their scarcest resource, moving from a multi-agency contingency model to an embedded RPO arrangement typically pays for itself within the first two or three senior hires in recovered engineering leadership time alone.

Comparing costs: RPO vs agencies in India

The cost comparison between RPO and recruitment agencies is less straightforward than it first appears, because the obvious comparison, placement fee versus monthly RPO fee, misses several cost components that matter in practice.

A contingency placement fee for a senior engineer in India typically runs 8 to 15 percent of annual cost to company. For a senior engineer on 40 lakhs per annum, that is a placement fee of 3.2 to 6 lakhs. Multiply that across five or six senior hires in a year and the agency fee bill is substantial. That number is also entirely divorced from what happened to the process along the way: the CVs reviewed, the interviews conducted, the offers rejected, and the time cost borne by the engineering team managing the search.

RPO pricing in India is structured around capacity rather than placement: monthly fees for embedded engagements, milestone-based fees for specific programmes, or per-hire fees at a lower percentage than contingency. The per-hire RPO rate typically runs 6 to 12 percent of CTC, below the contingency range, but the more meaningful comparison is total cost of hiring, not just the direct fee.

When you include the internal engineering manager time spent on CV review, briefing sessions with multiple agencies, interview scheduling and candidate communication, and offer management, the true cost of the multi-agency model is materially higher than the placement fees alone suggest. An embedded RPO partner absorbs much of that internal cost as part of the engagement. For companies making five or more senior hires per year, that absorption typically makes the RPO model cost-competitive or cheaper even before accounting for the quality improvement in the eventual hires.

The hybrid approach some companies use

The most sophisticated talent acquisition approaches in technology companies do not use a single model for all hiring. They match the model to the role level and the nature of the search, running different approaches in parallel for different parts of the organisation.

A common and effective structure uses a dedicated search model for VP level and above, where the stakes and the search complexity justify a bespoke, highly focused approach with a specialist executive search partner. Below that, an embedded RPO model handles senior individual contributor and director-level engineering roles, where passive sourcing capability and process consistency across multiple simultaneous searches matter most. Direct sourcing with internal TA capacity covers mid-level and junior roles, where active candidate pools exist and the cost-per-hire economics favour internal ownership.

Deciding which model to use at which level is itself a strategic decision that benefits from external perspective. The answer depends on the company's hiring volume, the seniority distribution of open roles, the internal TA capacity available, and the specific talent market dynamics for the company's locations and role types. Getting that structure right at the start of a growth phase is worth the time investment, because the wrong model compounds its costs over every hire made under it.
