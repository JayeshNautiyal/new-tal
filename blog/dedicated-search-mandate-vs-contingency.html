Dedicated Search vs Contingency Recruitment: Which Model Fits Your Senior Technical Hire

The choice between a dedicated search mandate and contingency recruitment is not about cost preference. It is about which model's incentive structure matches the kind of hire you are trying to make.

Most companies default to contingency recruitment without giving the decision much thought. A recruitment agency is engaged, they work for free unless a placement is made, and a fee is charged when someone joins. The model feels low-risk because there is no upfront cost. The risk is actually embedded in the incentive structure, and it becomes visible exactly when you most need the process to work: when you are trying to fill a senior technical role with a candidate who is not actively looking.

How contingency recruitment actually works

A contingency model means the agency is paid only on successful placement. The practical implication of this structure is that an agency working on contingency is never exclusively focused on your mandate. They are working across multiple clients simultaneously because the economics of contingency require volume: some mandates will close, others will not, and the agency's revenue depends on the total across the portfolio.

This creates a specific constraint: the agency focuses its limited sourcing time on candidates who are likely to close quickly and cleanly. An active candidate who is interviewing in multiple places and likely to accept an offer within two weeks is more valuable to a contingency recruiter's economics than a passive candidate who requires four weeks of relationship-building before they would even consider an exploratory conversation. The best hire for your role is often the second type. The contingency model systematically under-invests in finding them.

What a dedicated search mandate changes

A dedicated search mandate means the search partner commits exclusive or primary attention to your role in exchange for milestone-based fees tied to the progress of the search. The structure removes the economic incentive to deprioritise difficult-to-source passive candidates in favour of fast-moving active ones.

In practice, this changes the sourcing methodology. A dedicated search begins with a research phase: identifying the population of candidates who have the specific experience the role requires, regardless of whether those candidates are currently looking. The outreach is then built around that research rather than a database query for active candidates. This is a time-intensive approach, and it only makes economic sense for a search partner when they have a fee structure that compensates for that investment of time.

Milestone-based fees in a dedicated search context typically include an initiation payment when the engagement begins, a shortlist payment when a qualified shortlist is delivered, and a completion payment on successful placement. This structure ensures the search partner has a financial stake in every stage of the process, not just the final outcome.

When each model fits

Contingency recruitment is a good match for roles where there is a substantial pool of active candidates with the relevant skills. Mid-level software engineers with standard stack experience, junior data analysts, and support roles at well-defined levels are reasonable fits. The agency's database and network of active candidates is likely to contain relevant profiles, the search is not dependent on passive sourcing, and the contingency fee is a fair price for the administrative and matching work the agency does.

A dedicated search mandate becomes the appropriate model when one or more of the following is true. The role is at senior individual contributor level or above, where the best candidates are reliably employed and not actively looking. The role requires a specific combination of skills that is rare enough that a broad database search will produce few if any suitable candidates. The hiring timeline is fixed enough that a slow, unfocused search creates meaningful business risk. The role is significant enough that a poor hire would be costly to reverse.

Engineering leadership roles, senior AI engineering searches, and first-hire mandates for new functions or geographies almost always belong in the dedicated search category. The characteristics of those searches, passive talent, specialised skill requirements, and high stakes, are precisely the characteristics that contingency models handle poorly.

The conversation to have before engaging any search partner

Before engaging a search partner, it is worth being direct about the fee model and what it requires from both sides. Ask the partner to explain their sourcing methodology for this specific role. If the answer is primarily database and job board, the contingency model is probably appropriate and the right conversation is about candidate quality criteria. If the answer involves research-led passive sourcing, structured outreach, and a pipeline built from scratch, the conversation should include fee structure, exclusivity, and the timeline implications of that approach.

The fee structure is a signal about the incentive structure, and the incentive structure predicts the behaviour. A contingency arrangement with no upfront commitment will not produce a partner who invests heavily in a six-week passive sourcing campaign. A dedicated search arrangement with milestone fees will, because the economics support it. Matching the model to the hire is the first decision in any senior technical search, and getting it right before you start saves significant time and money compared to discovering the mismatch four weeks in.
