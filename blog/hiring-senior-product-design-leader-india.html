Hiring a Senior Product Design Leader in India: What the Search Actually Requires

Searches for Head of Design and VP of Product Design roles in India require a different brief and a different sourcing approach than engineering searches. The candidate pool, the assessment criteria, and the reasons searches fail are all distinct.

The market for experienced product design leaders in India has deepened significantly over the past decade, but it remains structurally smaller than the engineering talent market. Companies looking for a Head of Design or Director of Product Design for an India centre or product company are competing for a population of candidates who have built design teams in product contexts, who understand design systems and research operations, and who can represent design as a strategic function to product and engineering leadership. That population is not large, and the best candidates within it are typically not active in the job market.

What a strong design leadership brief looks like

A search for a senior product design leader requires more contextual detail in the brief than many hiring companies initially provide. The typical starting point is a job description written around tool familiarity and design process methodologies. That brief does not give a search partner enough to identify the right candidates, and it does not give candidates enough to evaluate the opportunity.

A strong brief for a design leadership search establishes the product context first: what the company builds, who uses it, what the design team's current scope is, and what the incoming leader would need to change or build. It covers the relationship between design and product management, because that relationship determines how much of the incoming leader's time is spent on strategic work versus execution. It specifies whether the role includes research ownership, content design, or design systems, as these are genuinely different disciplines that attract different candidate profiles.

The brief should also address what design maturity looks like at the company today. A design leader joining a team with strong foundations and a defined process brings a different set of skills to the role than one joining a company where design is still establishing its place in the product development cycle. Candidates assess these contextual factors carefully, and an honest brief that acknowledges the current state makes the sourcing conversation more credible.

Portfolio assessment versus CV screening

Design hiring has a direct evidence source that engineering hiring does not: the portfolio. A product design leader's portfolio of work is the primary signal of their craft quality and their approach to design problems. Assessing it correctly requires looking beyond visual polish at the process documentation, the problem framing, the constraints navigated, and the outcomes attributed to the work.

Senior design candidates typically present polished portfolios that show finished outcomes. The assessment conversation should go deeper: what problem was this design solving, what alternatives were considered and why were they rejected, how did user research inform the decisions shown, what changed after the first version shipped, and what would the candidate do differently now? A design leader who can answer these questions with specificity has genuine ownership of the work. One who answers primarily in terms of visual decisions and tool proficiency does not.

CV screening for design leadership roles fails for the same reasons it fails for senior engineering roles: the most experienced candidates are not optimising their CVs for keyword matching, and their depth of experience is not captured by a list of companies and role titles. The portfolio and the structured interview conversation are the primary assessment instruments.

Common mistakes in design leadership searches

The most common mistake is confusing craft seniority with leadership readiness. A designer who produces exceptional work as an individual contributor has a different skill set from one who has built, managed, and developed a team of designers. Both are valuable, but the role of Head of Design or VP of Product Design requires the second set of skills. Assessing for them means asking specifically about team structure decisions, hiring and onboarding approaches, how the candidate has handled underperformance, and how they develop junior and mid-level designers.

A second common mistake is treating salary benchmarking as a secondary concern. Experienced product design leaders in India have seen compensation levels rise substantially in the past few years, driven by the same supply-demand dynamics affecting engineering talent. A search that begins with a band calibrated to two-year-old market data will encounter candidates who are out of range at offer stage. This is a preventable problem if current market data is established at the brief stage.

The third mistake is running the search process too slowly. Senior design leaders who are genuinely passive in their job search are evaluating the hiring process as a signal of how the company makes decisions. A process that takes six weeks to move from first interview to offer — with no communication in between — tells a candidate something about the organisation that a compelling job brief cannot undo. Design leaders in particular tend to be thoughtful about the environments they join, and a slow or disorganised process is a real risk to closing the best candidates.

Reference conversations for design leadership

References for design leadership hires should include a former product management peer, an engineering lead who worked alongside the candidate, a designer who reported to them, and where possible a former executive who oversaw the design function. The combination gives a three-dimensional view of the candidate's craft quality, their cross-functional relationships, their leadership of the team under them, and their upward communication of design strategy.

Questions to ask: how did this person change the way design operated in the organisation, what was the hardest decision they made as a design leader and how did they make it, what would you not want them working on and why, and how did they handle a situation where engineering or product pushed back on a design recommendation? These questions produce specific answers that a competent reference can give and that a generic character reference cannot.
