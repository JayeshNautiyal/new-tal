How to Write an Engineering Hiring Brief That Produces Better Shortlists

The hiring brief is the most leveraged document in any engineering search. A well-written brief produces a strong shortlist in 10 days. A vague one produces three rounds of wrong candidates and a frustrated hiring manager. Here is how to write one that works.

Talhive Oct 29, 2026

Every poor engineering search pipeline can be traced back to a brief that did not give the recruiter what they needed. Not a lack of effort. Not the wrong sourcing channels. Not an impossible market. A brief that was too vague, too generic, or too focused on what the company wanted without addressing what would attract a strong candidate to want it in return.

The hiring brief is where the quality of a search is determined. A recruiter executing against a precise brief with a clear sourcing strategy will outperform the same recruiter working from a vague one every time, regardless of how hard they work. The brief is the ceiling. The search cannot produce a better shortlist than the brief allows for.

Why most hiring briefs fail

Most job descriptions are written from the company's perspective: a list of requirements, responsibilities, and qualifications that define what the company wants. They are compliance documents and communications documents, and they serve both functions adequately.

A hiring brief that produces results must do something different. It must capture not just what the company wants, but why a strong candidate at a competing employer would want to leave for this role. These are different documents with different purposes. A job description tells the recruiter what to screen for. A hiring brief tells them how to find, attract, and assess the best available candidates.

The confusion between the two is the most common source of poor searches. When a hiring manager hands the recruiter a job description and considers the brief complete, the recruiter is working without the strategic information they need. The search will be generic because the brief was generic.

The five components of a brief that produces results

  • The Problem Statement

    What specific problem is this hire solving? Not the role title and not the list of responsibilities. The actual engineering or organisational problem that will remain unsolved if this role is not filled. The more specific and concrete this is, the better the search will be.

  • The Team Context

    Who will this person work with, report to, and be evaluated by? What is the dynamic of the engineering team they are joining? What kinds of people thrive there and what kinds struggle? This context shapes who the recruiter is looking for beyond the technical profile.

  • The Technical Requirements

    What must they be able to do, have done before, and think about? Separated explicitly into must-haves (genuine non-negotiables) and strong preferences. Most briefs conflate these. The separation is essential for calibration.

  • The Company Differentiator

    Why would a strong candidate at Razorpay, Atlassian, or CRED consider this role? The honest, specific case for why this opportunity is worth their attention. Not marketing language. The genuine differentiators this company offers on scope, challenge, trajectory, and team.

  • The Non-Negotiables vs Nice-to-Haves

    What would actually stop an offer if absent? Most briefs list 12 to 15 requirements and treat all of them as essential. In practice, the genuine must-haves for most senior engineering roles are three or four. The rest are strong preferences. Knowing the difference allows the recruiter to screen efficiently and surface candidates who are a strong fit for what actually matters.

Writing the problem statement

The problem statement is the section that most hiring briefs either omit or get wrong. The wrong version: "We are looking for a senior backend engineer to join our growing team." This tells the recruiter nothing about why this role exists, what problem it solves, or what a successful hire will be doing in six months.

The right version states the problem concretely:

Weak Problem Statement

"We are looking for a senior backend engineer to join our growing engineering team and help us scale our platform."

Strong Problem Statement

"We are building a real-time payments reconciliation system that needs to handle 5 million transactions per day with sub-100ms latency. Our current architecture has a bottleneck at the database write layer. We need an engineer who has solved this specific class of distributed systems problem before and can lead the redesign."

The specificity serves two functions. First, it attracts the right candidates: engineers who have solved that specific problem will self-identify. Second, it repels the wrong ones: engineers without relevant experience will not apply or accept outreach, saving screening time. Specificity is both a quality filter and an efficiency mechanism.

Writing the technical requirements without creating a wishlist

The wishlist problem is one of the most consistent patterns in poor engineering searches. A brief that lists fifteen "must-have" requirements produces near-zero qualifying candidates in practice, because the intersection of all fifteen is essentially empty in any realistic talent pool.

The discipline of separating must-haves from preferences requires the hiring manager to make a specific decision about each requirement: if a candidate had everything except this, would you still interview them? If the honest answer is yes, it is not a must-have. Most requirements that feel essential turn out, under this test, to be strong preferences.

For most senior engineering roles in India, the genuine must-haves are three or four items. Everything else is a preference of varying strength. Knowing this distinction allows the recruiter to screen against what actually matters and to present candidates who are a strong match on the things that are truly non-negotiable, even if they have gaps on the preferences.

Writing the company differentiator

This is the most neglected section of most engineering hiring briefs. Companies write at length about what they need and say almost nothing about what they offer that a strong candidate does not already have at their current employer.

Senior engineers in India are evaluating your company against well-known alternatives. The recruiter needs to be able to make a specific, honest case for why your opportunity is worth a senior engineer's attention before that engineer has agreed to a conversation. Generic claims about exciting challenges and fast growth do not provide that case. They are indistinguishable from every other outreach message the engineer receives.

The differentiator section should cover: the scope of ownership the incoming engineer will have, the specific technical problem they will get to solve, the calibre of the team they will join, the company's trajectory and how that translates to growth for the engineer personally, and what the equity situation actually looks like. These are the dimensions engineers use to evaluate opportunities, and the brief should give the recruiter specific and accurate information on each.

Be honest. A candidate who joins on a premise that turns out to be false leaves quickly. That is the worst outcome for everyone, and it starts with a company differentiator section that overstated what was on offer.

Frequently Asked Questions

A well-written engineering hiring brief is typically one to two pages. Long enough to cover all five components properly, short enough to be useful. The goal is not comprehensiveness for its own sake but precision: every sentence should give the recruiter actionable information they can use to source, screen, or pitch the role. A brief that runs to five pages is usually a job description in disguise.
The hiring brief should be written collaboratively between the hiring manager and the recruiter in a working session. The hiring manager owns the problem statement, team context, and technical requirements. The recruiter contributes the company differentiator angle and the candidate motivation framing. A brief written by HR alone, without input from the hiring manager or recruiter, typically produces a compliance document rather than a search strategy.
The brief should be reviewed and updated after every shortlist calibration session. If the first three candidates consistently miss on a specific dimension, that dimension is either misspecified in the brief or difficult to find in the current market. Both require an update. A brief treated as fixed for the duration of a 12-week search will produce a search that drifts progressively further from what the hiring manager actually wants.

A job description describes what you want. A hiring brief explains where to find it, how to attract it, and how to assess it. Both are useful. Confusing one for the other produces poor searches.
