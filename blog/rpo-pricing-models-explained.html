RPO Pricing Models Explained: Per-Hire, Monthly Capacity, and Milestone-Based Fees

RPO pricing is less opaque than agency fees once you understand the three main models. Each has a different risk and incentive profile. Getting this right at the start of an engagement saves significant budget and avoids a misaligned commercial arrangement.

Talhive Oct 28, 2026

Why RPO pricing differs from agency fees

The most common confusion when companies first engage with Recruitment Process Outsourcing is expecting the pricing to follow the same logic as a contingency recruitment agency. Agency fees are straightforward: a percentage of the hired candidate's salary, paid when the placement is made. RPO pricing does not follow that structure, and the difference is not cosmetic. It reflects a fundamentally different commercial relationship.

A contingency agency fee rewards the placement event. The agency is incentivised to create that event as quickly as possible. RPO pricing rewards process ownership and sustained hiring capability. The provider is incentivised to build a search that produces the right candidate, because their commercial model is not tied to the speed of individual closures. Understanding this distinction before you begin evaluating RPO providers means you can assess the commercial structure alongside the methodology, rather than trying to compare incompatible models.

There are three main RPO pricing models: per-hire, monthly capacity, and milestone-based fees. Each has different risk and incentive characteristics, and each fits a different kind of hiring programme. Most companies do not need to choose one model for all their hiring. The right structure often varies by role level, search complexity, and hiring volume within a single engagement.

The per-hire RPO model

The per-hire model charges a fixed fee for each successful placement, agreed in advance of the search. Unlike a contingency agency fee (which is a percentage and therefore variable), the per-hire RPO fee is typically a fixed amount or a percentage agreed at a lower rate than standard contingency. The client pays nothing until a hire is made.

The per-hire model is the most familiar to companies coming from an agency background, and it provides the clearest risk profile: cost is directly tied to outcomes. This makes it an appropriate starting point for companies that want to test an RPO engagement without committing to a sustained capacity arrangement, or for companies whose hiring volume is genuinely episodic and hard to forecast.

The limitation of the per-hire model is that it retains some of the incentive tension of contingency hiring. If the fee is only paid on placement, the provider still has a financial incentive to close the search rather than extend it in pursuit of a marginally better candidate. The per-hire RPO model mitigates this compared to contingency (because the fee is not percentage-driven and the provider is typically expected to deliver a qualified shortlist before the fee is earned), but it does not eliminate the tension entirely.

Per-hire RPO works best for: episodic hiring programmes where volume is not predictable, companies evaluating RPO as a model for the first time, and individual searches where the scope is well-defined and the expected time-to-fill is short. It is less well suited to sustained programmes where the process consistency benefits of the embedded model depend on a committed capacity arrangement.

The monthly capacity model

The monthly capacity model is the most common structure for embedded RPO engagements. The client commits to a monthly fee in exchange for a defined capacity: typically a specified number of active searches and the full-time or fractional attention of an embedded specialist recruiter.

The commercial logic of the monthly capacity model aligns incentives correctly. The provider is not rewarded for closing placements quickly; they are rewarded for managing a high-quality process that produces good hires over the duration of the engagement. The embedded recruiter can invest appropriate time in passive candidate relationships, in brief development and calibration, and in the sourcing thesis work that defines the quality of the eventual shortlist, without the financial pressure to rush to a close.

For the client, the monthly capacity model provides cost predictability and a clear understanding of what is being purchased. The fee buys a defined resource and a defined methodology, not a variable cost tied to the number of placements made in a given month. This makes budget planning straightforward and removes the per-hire fee anxiety that can distort hiring decisions late in a search process.

The monthly capacity model is sometimes called a dedicated search commitment, reflecting the committed nature of the resource and the dedicated search approach it funds. It is distinct from a contingency arrangement in both structure and intent: the provider is a committed partner for the duration of the engagement rather than a vendor competing with others to close a placement first.

Monthly capacity arrangements are most appropriate for: companies with ongoing or predictable hiring needs, three to eight active searches per quarter at senior level, and a preference for process consistency across multiple simultaneous searches. For a startup scaling its engineering team over two to three quarters, this is typically the structure that delivers the best combination of sourcing quality and cost efficiency.

Milestone-based fees

The milestone-based structure ties fees to defined points in the search process rather than to a single placement event or a monthly commitment. A typical milestone structure might be: a portion of the fee paid at engagement initiation and brief sign-off, a portion on delivery of a qualified shortlist, and the balance on offer acceptance or candidate start date.

The milestone model balances the client's interest in managing risk with the provider's interest in being compensated for substantive work done. Paying at brief sign-off and shortlist delivery means the provider is recognised for the search work that produces a qualified shortlist, not just the final placement. It also means the client is paying for demonstrated progress rather than committing the full fee upfront with no checkpoints.

Milestone-based fees are common in project RPO engagements and in specialist or senior searches where the expected timeline is longer and the search complexity is higher. For a company commissioning a specific senior engineering leadership hire, or building a new function that requires sourcing a group of specialist engineers simultaneously, a milestone structure provides clear commercial checkpoints without the per-hire pressure of pure contingency or the open-ended commitment of a monthly capacity model.

For executive and leadership searches at VP level and above, milestone-based or dedicated search structures are the industry standard. The dedicated search model commits the provider exclusively to the client's search for the duration of the engagement and is incompatible with contingency, because the exclusivity and depth of commitment that senior searches require cannot be economically justified without an upfront commitment from the client.

What RPO services in India typically cost

The range of RPO pricing in India is genuinely broad, because it reflects significant variation in the seniority of the recruiter, the depth of the methodology, the number of active searches included, and the market coverage the provider offers.

For embedded RPO engagements focused on senior engineering roles, monthly capacity fees for a specialist embedded recruiter with strong India engineering market depth will be at the higher end of the range versus a generalist provider. The right frame is not what is the cheapest RPO option but what is the total cost of the alternative. A contingency agency placement fee for a senior engineer on 40 to 50 lakhs per annum is 3.5 to 7.5 lakhs per hire. Multiply that across five to seven senior hires in a year and the agency fee programme is a significant number, with no process ownership, no pipeline visibility, and no institutional knowledge building to show for it.

RPO services in India priced on a per-hire basis typically run 6 to 12 percent of annual CTC, below the standard contingency range. Monthly capacity arrangements vary based on scope. The honest comparison the client should make is RPO total cost versus the total cost of alternatives, where total cost includes: agency fees, internal engineering manager time spent on briefing sessions and CV review, offer management overhead, and the cost of a hire that was not quite right and has to be repeated six months later.

For companies making six or more senior hires per year, that total cost comparison almost always favours embedded RPO. For companies making two or three senior hires per year, the comparison is closer and depends on the specific roles and the internal capacity available to manage the process.

How to structure an RPO budget conversation

The most productive way to approach the RPO budget conversation is to start with the hiring plan rather than with a fee ceiling. Define the roles you need to fill, the seniority levels, the expected timeline, and the internal capacity available to support the process. From that picture, a good RPO provider can give you a clear breakdown of which pricing model fits your situation and what the expected cost looks like across your full hiring programme.

The questions worth asking in a pricing conversation with an RPO provider include: what is included in the monthly fee and what is additional? How are additional searches handled if the hiring plan expands? What happens if a placement does not work out within the guarantee period? How is the fee structured if a search is placed on hold?

An embedded RPO engagement is a commercial relationship that will run for months. Getting the fee structure right at the start is worth the time it takes. A provider who cannot give you a clear and transparent answer to those questions is telling you something useful before the engagement begins.
