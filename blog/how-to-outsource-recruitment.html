How to Outsource Recruitment Without Losing Control of Hiring Quality

Outsourcing recruitment is not the same as outsourcing the hiring decision. Done correctly, an RPO model gives you more visibility and control over your hiring process, not less. Here is how to set it up properly.

Talhive October 2024

The most common fear about outsourcing recruitment is that quality will slip when the process moves outside the building. The fear is understandable, and it is grounded in real experience: most engineering leaders who have used contingency agencies have received CVs that missed the brief, dealt with candidates who were oversold on the role, or watched a placement leave within six months because the company was described inaccurately.

But those experiences are failures of the agency model, not failures of outsourcing itself. The problem is not that the work moved outside. The problem is that the model used did not include adequate governance, shared accountability, or the right incentive structure. Recruitment Process Outsourcing services built on the embedded model solve these problems structurally. The governance question becomes: how do you set it up correctly?

The fear behind the question

Most engineering leaders who are cautious about outsourcing recruitment have a specific mental image: a recruiter they have never met, working at a firm with ten other clients, sending CVs that were not screened properly, collecting a fee if one of them happens to get hired. That image is accurate for the contingency agency model.

RPO done well looks nothing like this. An embedded RPO partner works inside your organisation. They attend your standups. They know the hiring managers personally. They understand the difference between what the job description says and what the team actually needs. The structural difference between this and a contingency agency relationship is as significant as the difference between an employee and a vendor. Same task, completely different operating model.

RPO done poorly, however, can replicate the contingency agency problems at larger scale. The governance model is what separates the two. A well-run outsourced recruitment function gives you more visibility into your hiring pipeline than most internal TA teams achieve. A poorly run one gives you the illusion of activity and a high volume of unsuitable candidates.

Define what you are outsourcing and what you are not

Before an engagement starts, both parties need a shared and explicit understanding of where the boundary sits. The confusion about this boundary is the source of most RPO quality failures.

What you are outsourcing: sourcing, screening, pipeline management, candidate experience, interview scheduling, offer management, and pipeline reporting. These are the process functions. Moving them outside is sensible if the embedded partner has more capacity and skill at them than your internal team.

What you are not outsourcing: the hiring decision, the culture evaluation, the technical assessment design, and the judgement about whether a specific candidate meets your standard. These are the decision functions. They stay inside, with the hiring managers who own the roles and understand the team.

The boundary matters precisely. An embedded RPO partner who understands this boundary makes hiring managers more effective by handling the process so they can focus on evaluation. One who blurs it by recommending hires rather than presenting assessments creates a dynamic where the hiring manager feels they are rubber-stamping rather than deciding. Setting up governance that works

Governance in an embedded RPO engagement is lighter than most people expect, because the embedded partner is inside the communication loop rather than outside it. The key mechanisms are straightforward.

Brief development jointly, not separately. The sourcing thesis for each role is written by the embedded recruiter with direct input from the hiring manager in a working session, not handed over as a job description and interpreted independently. Joint brief development means both parties agree on what a good candidate looks like before sourcing begins, which prevents the most common calibration failure.

Weekly pipeline reviews that calibrate, not just report. The pipeline review is not a status update where the recruiter tells you how many candidates are at each stage. It is a calibration session where the hiring manager gives specific feedback on why candidates who were shortlisted did or did not pass. That feedback directly improves the sourcing thesis and the screening criteria. Without this calibration loop, a search can drift for weeks in the wrong direction.

Clear escalation protocol. When a candidate is borderline, who decides? Establish this before the first shortlist arrives. A clear protocol prevents situations where a good candidate stalls in the process because neither the recruiter nor the hiring manager is sure who owns the next step.

The right metrics. Volume of CVs submitted is not a quality metric. It is a noise metric. The metrics that tell you whether an embedded RPO engagement is working are: quality of shortlist (measured by shortlist-to-interview conversion rate), time-to-shortlist from brief sign-off, and offer acceptance rate. A shortlist where four out of five candidates pass to interview is dramatically better than one where two out of fifteen do, even if the latter feels more active.

How to evaluate shortlist quality without drowning in CVs

A properly run embedded RPO engagement delivers an assessed shortlist, not a CV dump. The difference is in how each candidate is presented. A CV dump hands you a profile and expects you to form a view from scratch. An assessed shortlist gives you a written candidate summary covering: what they have built that is relevant to this role, what their stated motivations are for considering a move, where the genuine concerns are, and a clear recommendation from the embedded recruiter on whether to advance to a first interview.

The hiring manager's job at shortlist review is to evaluate those recommendations and provide calibration feedback, not to re-screen from scratch. When this is working correctly, a hiring manager can review a shortlist of five assessed candidates in 60 to 90 minutes and give specific enough feedback for the recruiter to adjust the sourcing strategy immediately.

If you are spending more than 30 minutes per candidate at shortlist review, the shortlist has not been assessed enough before delivery. That is the recruiter's problem to fix, not the hiring manager's to absorb.

Protecting candidate quality during high-volume periods

The most common quality failure in outsourced recruitment happens when hiring accelerates. A company that was running two or three searches simultaneously suddenly needs to run eight or ten. The pressure to fill roles quickly creates a temptation to lower the screening bar or skip calibration steps. Candidates who would not have made a previous shortlist start appearing on the next one.

A contingency agency under this pressure moves faster because their fee depends on placement speed, and quality is not their accountability. An embedded RPO partner holds the bar because their accountability is to a long-term relationship, not to individual placement events. The fee structure removes the incentive to submit a fast candidate over a good one.

This structural difference is especially important when hiring volume increases rapidly. The embedded model is specifically designed for the scenario where a company needs to scale hiring without scaling compromise.

Candidate NPS

Tracked

Candidate satisfaction with the process

When time-to-shortlist is consistently above 14 days, it is usually a brief development problem: the sourcing thesis was not specific enough to identify relevant candidates quickly. When shortlist-to-interview conversion is below 60%, it is usually a calibration problem: the recruiter's interpretation of the brief diverges from the hiring manager's. When offer acceptance falls below 75%, it is usually a candidate experience or expectation management problem: candidates reaching the offer stage who were not properly prepared for what was being offered.

Each of these failures has a specific fix. Tracking the metrics is what makes the fix visible rather than leaving the problem to accumulate silently.
