Why CV Screening and Coding Tests Fail Senior AI Hires

Companies routinely apply junior-level screening methods to senior AI engineering searches and then wonder why the shortlists are weak. The tools are not broken; they are just pointed at the wrong signals.

The hiring process most companies use for software engineers was designed in an era when the challenge was volume: how do you efficiently filter hundreds of applicants down to the ten worth interviewing? The toolkit that emerged from that problem — CV keyword matching, a take-home coding challenge, and a structured interview loop — is reasonable for that purpose.

Senior AI engineering is not that problem. The challenge is not filtering a large volume of applicants. The challenge is identifying a small number of engineers who have genuinely built and operated ML systems at scale, reaching them despite the fact that they are not actively looking, and assessing their real capabilities rather than their ability to perform on proxy tasks.

Applying volume-filtering tools to a scarcity-and-depth problem produces predictably bad outcomes: long pipelines, weak shortlists, and hires who perform on paper but struggle in production.

The CV keyword problem

A CV keyword filter works when the skill you are screening for correlates with the presence of certain words. For junior engineers who have recently completed bootcamps or degrees, this is often true. They list frameworks, certifications, and languages because those qualifications are the main signal they can offer.

Senior AI engineers do not typically write CVs this way. They describe what they built, the problems they solved, and the scale at which they operated. A senior engineer who spent three years building production recommendation systems at a major e-commerce company may not have "PyTorch" or "MLflow" in their CV, despite having used both extensively. A candidate who just completed an AI certification almost certainly will.

The filter systematically favours recency and intentional CV optimisation over depth and actual experience. The candidates who pass are often not the candidates you need.

Why timed coding tests do not predict senior AI performance

The argument for coding tests is that they provide a standardised, objective signal. That argument has some merit at the junior to mid level, where the ability to solve algorithmic problems quickly under time pressure is at least partially correlated with the skills the role requires.

At senior AI engineering level, the correlation breaks down. The skills that make a senior AI engineer effective are not measured by a timed algorithmic test. They include: designing ML system architecture under production constraints, debugging model degradation in live systems, building inference pipelines that handle variable load without latency blowouts, making data quality decisions quickly when the pipeline is broken, and communicating system design tradeoffs to product and engineering leadership.

None of these capabilities are well proxied by the ability to reverse a binary tree in 20 minutes. A senior engineer who has been focused on production ML work for five years will often perform worse on a LeetCode-style problem than a mid-level engineer who has been practising algorithmic problems. Screening by the latter to hire for the former produces shortlists that have the wrong shape entirely.

There is also a second-order effect: the best senior AI engineers are aware that timed coding tests are a poor fit for their experience level, and they disengage from hiring processes that lead with them. The candidates most willing to invest in a LeetCode performance are often the ones most focused on optimising their hiring performance rather than their engineering output.

What evidence-based assessment actually looks like

A data-driven candidate assessment framework for senior AI roles shifts from proxies to direct evidence. The assessment is built around what the candidate has actually done, not what they can do under test conditions.

The structured technical interview replaces algorithmic tests with case analysis of real systems. A candidate is asked to walk through the most complex production ML system they have owned: the architecture, the data pipeline, the deployment approach, the monitoring strategy, and the specific failures they encountered and resolved. The interviewer is not looking for a textbook answer; they are looking for the specificity, depth, and ownership that indicates the candidate was actually driving decisions rather than executing someone else's design.

Talhive's CDQ (Career Discovery Questionnaire) methodology goes beyond the technical dimension to map the candidate's motivations, trajectory, and working style. Senior hires fail for cultural and contextual reasons as often as technical ones. Understanding how a candidate approaches ambiguity, how they communicate with non-technical stakeholders, and what kind of engineering environment they perform best in is as important as validating their ML credentials.

Reference calls are treated as evidence, not formality. Structured conversations with a former engineering manager, a peer engineer, and where possible a direct report produce a third-party perspective on the candidate's actual production performance that no interview can replicate. Questions are specific: what was the hardest technical problem this person solved while working with you, what did they struggle with, what would you change about how they approached a specific challenge?

A practical checklist for engineering leaders

If you are running an AI engineering assessment internally, these markers separate strong candidates from those who are strong at presenting as strong candidates:

  • Can the candidate name a specific production system they owned and describe the architecture in detail, including the decisions they made and the alternatives they considered?
  • Can they describe a production failure, their diagnostic process, and the root cause in specific technical terms?
  • Can they explain a model performance degradation they experienced, what caused it, and how they addressed it?
  • Can they describe the inference infrastructure they have operated, including the scale, the latency requirements, and any optimisation work they did?
  • Can they explain how they made a data quality decision under time pressure, with imperfect information?
  • Do references from people who worked directly with them confirm the depth of technical ownership the candidate describes?

A candidate who answers these questions in specifics has earned their seniority claim. A candidate who answers in generalities has not, regardless of how well their CV reads or how they perform on a coding test.

The investment required to run this kind of assessment is real, but it is modest compared to the cost of a failed senior AI hire. Getting the assessment right is the most important lever in the process.
